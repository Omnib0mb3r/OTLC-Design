# COPPA: Children's Online Privacy Protection Act (15 U.S.C. 6501-6506) and the COPPA Rule (16 CFR Part 312)

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| **Jurisdiction** | United States, federal. Protects children under 13. Reaches any commercial operator, including one based outside the US, whose site or service is operated for commercial purposes "involving commerce among the several States or with one or more foreign nations" and is directed to children or collects from a known child (16 CFR 312.2, "Operator"). |
| **Regulator / enforcer** | Federal Trade Commission. Civil penalties are obtained by the Department of Justice on FTC referral. State attorneys general may also bring COPPA actions (the 2019 Google/YouTube action was a joint FTC and New York AG matter). |
| **In force since** | Statute enacted 1998 (15 U.S.C. 6501-6506; the Rule's authority note). Rule issued November 3, 1999, original effective date **April 21, 2000**. Last amended before 2025 in January 2013 (published January 17, 2013, effective July 1, 2013). Confirmed from the preamble to FR doc. 2025-05904 as served by govinfo.gov. |
| **Last amended** | Final rule amendments published April 22, 2025 (FR doc. 2025-05904, RIN 3084-AB20). Effective June 23, 2025. General compliance date April 22, 2026. Safe harbor provisions 312.11(d)(1), (d)(4), and (g) run on earlier clocks written into the rule text itself: 312.11(d)(4) public posting "no later than July 21, 2025", and 312.11(d)(1) annual reporting "by October 22, 2025, and annually thereafter". |
| **Last reviewed** | 2026-07-20 |
| **Status** | In force, fully. The April 22, 2026 compliance date has passed. Layered on top: an FTC Enforcement Policy Statement dated February 25, 2026 that narrows enforcement for age-verification data only, discussed under "Notes". |

## Applies when

Answer these at intake. COPPA has **no revenue threshold, no employee count, and no record count**. A one-person studio shipping a single kids' game is caught on exactly the same terms as Disney.

**Step 1: is the operator covered at all?**

- [ ] The site, web app, mobile app, connected toy, voice assistant skill, game, ad network, analytics provider, or plugin is **operated for commercial purposes**. "Operator" in 16 CFR 312.2 explicitly excludes "any nonprofit entity that would otherwise be exempt from coverage under Section 5 of the Federal Trade Commission Act." A genuinely exempt nonprofit is outside the Rule. A nonprofit operating for the profit of members, or a commercial arm of a nonprofit, generally is not exempt. Get this confirmed in writing, do not assume from the ".org".
- [ ] Personal information is collected or maintained **by or on behalf of** the operator. Under 312.2, information is collected on the operator's behalf when "it is collected or maintained by an agent or service provider of the operator" **or** when "the operator benefits by allowing another person to collect personal information directly from users." That second clause is the one that catches an embedded ad SDK: the site owner is on the hook for what the SDK collects.

**Step 2: is the property child-directed, mixed audience, or general audience?**

This is the decision that drives the whole build. Record the answer and the reasoning in the project file.

- [ ] **Child-directed (primary audience under 13).** 16 CFR 312.2 defines "website or online service directed to children" as a commercial site or service, "or portion thereof, that is targeted to children." The Commission weighs: subject matter, visual content, use of animated characters or child-oriented activities and incentives, music or other audio content, age of models, presence of child celebrities or celebrities who appeal to children, language or other characteristics, and whether advertising promoting or appearing on the site is directed to children. The 2025 amendments added a second sentence: the Commission "will also consider competent and reliable empirical evidence regarding audience composition and evidence regarding the intended audience, including **marketing or promotional materials or plans, representations to consumers or to third parties, reviews by users or third parties, and the age of users on similar websites or services**." Practical consequence: the client's own pitch deck, media kit, app store category, and ad-buy targeting are evidence against them. If the client sells the property to advertisers as reaching kids, it is child-directed.
- [ ] **A portion of the site is child-directed.** The definition says "or portion thereof." A general-audience brand site with a kids' games subdirectory is caught as to that subdirectory. Scope it as its own property in the build.
- [ ] **Mixed audience.** New standalone category in the 2025 amendments. 16 CFR 312.2 defines a "mixed audience website or online service" as one "that is directed to children under the criteria set forth in paragraph (1) of the definition of website or online service directed to children, but that does **not** target children as its primary audience, and does not collect personal information from any visitor, other than for the limited purposes set forth in 312.5(c), prior to collecting age information or using another means that is reasonably calculated, in light of available technology, to determine whether the visitor is a child." Two-step test: first is it child-directed at all, then does it target children as its *primary* audience. Only if the answer is yes-then-no is it mixed audience.
- [ ] **Actual knowledge of a child user.** Even a pure general-audience site is caught the moment it has actual knowledge it is collecting personal information from a child under 13. Actual knowledge, not constructive. A birth-date field, a support ticket saying "I'm 11", or a school-grade dropdown can create it.
- [ ] **Actual knowledge of collecting from a child-directed property (the ad network / SDK / plugin trigger).** Paragraph (2) of the definition: "A website or online service shall be deemed directed to children when it has actual knowledge that it is collecting personal information directly from users of another website or online service directed to children." If the studio builds an embeddable widget, an analytics tool, or an ad integration and knows a customer's property is kid-directed, the studio's own service becomes child-directed as to that traffic.

**Step 3: is "personal information" actually collected?**

16 CFR 312.2 lists eleven categories. Any one triggers the Rule:

1. First and last name.
2. Home or other physical address including street name and name of a city or town.
3. Online contact information (email address or substantially similar identifier permitting direct contact, including IM ID, VOIP ID, video chat ID, or a mobile number used only for parental-consent texts).
4. A screen or user name where it functions in the same manner as online contact information.
5. Telephone number.
6. **A government-issued identifier**, such as a Social Security, State identification card, birth certificate, or passport number.
7. **A persistent identifier that can be used to recognize a user over time and across different websites or online services**, including "a customer number held in a cookie, an Internet Protocol (IP) address, a processor or device serial number, or unique device identifier."
8. A photograph, video, or audio file where the file contains a child's image or voice.
9. Geolocation information sufficient to identify street name and name of a city or town.
10. **A biometric identifier that can be used for the automated or semi-automated recognition of an individual**, such as fingerprints, handprints, retina patterns, iris patterns, genetic data including a DNA sequence, voiceprints, gait patterns, facial templates, or faceprints.
11. Information about the child or the child's parents that the operator collects online from the child and combines with any identifier above.

Categories 6 and 10 were added by the 2025 amendments. **Category 7 is the one that catches ordinary web builds**: a cookie ID, an IP address, or a device ID is personal information. There is no "we only use analytics" carve-out. A kid-directed page with Google Analytics on it is collecting personal information from children.

"Collects" is also broad (312.2): requesting, prompting, or encouraging a child to submit personal information online; enabling a child to make personal information publicly available in identifiable form; **or passive tracking of a child online**.

**Who is NOT caught**

- Nonprofit entities that would otherwise be exempt from FTC Act Section 5.
- General-audience sites with no actual knowledge of child users and no child-directed portion.
- A mixed audience site **with respect to any visitor not identified as under 13**. Paragraph (3) of the directed-to-children definition: "A mixed audience website or online service shall not be deemed directed to children with regard to any visitor not identified as under 13." This is the payoff for building the age screen: adults on the same property are outside COPPA.
- A site that merely links to a kids' site. Paragraph (4): a site "shall not be deemed directed to children solely because it refers or links to a commercial website or online service directed to children by using information location tools, including a directory, index, reference, pointer, or hypertext link."
- Teens 13 to 17. COPPA stops at the thirteenth birthday. Duties to older minors come from other files in this library (California Eraser Button, state design codes, state privacy statutes). A site can be fully COPPA-compliant and still owe those.

## What the site must have

| Requirement | What that means in the build |
|---|---|
| **Online privacy notice for children (312.4(d))** | A page stating: (1) name, address, telephone number, and email address of **all** operators collecting or maintaining personal information from children through the service (one may be designated to answer parent inquiries, but every operator must still be named); (2) what information is collected, including whether the service lets a child make personal information publicly available, how it is used, the disclosure practices **including "the identities and specific categories of any third parties to which the operator discloses personal information and the purposes for such disclosures"**, and the operator's data retention policy required by 312.10; (3) if a persistent identifier is collected under the 312.5(c)(7) internal-operations exception, the specific internal operations it is collected for and the means used to ensure it is not used to contact a specific individual, amass a profile, or for any other purpose; (4) if audio files containing a child's voice are collected under 312.5(c)(9), how they are used and that they are deleted immediately after responding; (5) if applicable, that the parent can review or delete the child's information and refuse further collection, plus the procedures. |
| **Prominent link placement (312.4(d))** | A "prominent and clearly labeled link" to that notice **on the home or landing page or screen**, and **at each area where personal information is collected from children**, "in close proximity to the requests for information in each such area." A general-audience site with a separate children's area must post the link on the home or landing page of the children's area. Build consequence: the footer link alone is not enough. Every form, every signup, every upload control on a kid-directed screen needs its own adjacent link. |
| **Direct notice to the parent (312.4(b) and (c))** | A separate notice delivered to the parent before collection, not the same document as the online policy. For the consent flow (312.4(c)(1)) it must state: that the operator collected the parent's or child's online contact information to get consent; that consent is required and nothing will be collected, used, or disclosed without it; the items of personal information to be collected, how they will be used, and the potential disclosure opportunities; where information goes to third parties, **the identities or specific categories of those third parties and the purposes, plus a statement that the parent can consent to collection and use without consenting to third-party disclosure** except where disclosure is integral to the service; a hyperlink to the online notice; how the parent gives verifiable consent; and that if the parent does not respond in a reasonable time, the operator will delete the contact information and names from its records. Separate, shorter content lists apply to the voluntary-notice, multiple-contact, and child-safety flows (312.4(c)(2) through (c)(4)). |
| **Verifiable parental consent before any collection (312.5(a)(1))** | Consent must be obtained "before any collection, use, or disclosure of personal information from children, including consent to any material change in the collection, use, or disclosure practices to which the parent has previously consented." Material change means re-consent, not a changelog entry. |
| **Separate consent for non-integral third-party disclosure (312.5(a)(2))** | "An operator must give the parent the option to consent to the collection and use of the child's personal information without consenting to disclosure of his or her personal information to third parties, unless such disclosure is integral to the website or online service. An operator required to give the parent this option must obtain **separate** verifiable parental consent to such disclosure." Build consequence: the consent UI needs two independent affirmative choices, and the second must be genuinely refusable without breaking the core service. One combined checkbox fails. Targeted advertising is the paradigm case of a non-integral disclosure. |
| **An approved consent method (312.5(b)(2))** | The enumerated methods are: (i) a consent form signed by the parent and returned by postal mail, fax, or electronic scan; (ii) requiring the parent, in connection with a transaction, to use a credit card, debit card, or other online payment system that notifies the primary account holder of each discrete transaction; (iii) a call to a toll-free number staffed by trained personnel; (iv) connecting to trained personnel by video conference; (v) checking a government-issued ID against databases, with the ID deleted from records promptly after verification; (vi) knowledge-based authentication using dynamic multiple-choice questions with a low probability of correct guessing and difficulty such that a child 12 or younger in the parent's household could not reasonably ascertain the answers; (vii) a government-issued photographic ID verified authentic and compared by facial recognition against a live phone or webcam image, confirmed by trained personnel, with ID and images promptly deleted; (viii) **email plus** additional steps (confirmatory email, or obtaining a postal address or phone number and confirming by letter or call), **available only to an operator that does not "disclose" children's personal information as defined in 312.2**; (ix) **text message plus** the same kind of additional steps, subject to the same no-disclosure limit. An FTC-approved safe harbor program may approve other methods for its members (312.5(b)(3)). |
| **Watch the email-plus and text-plus limit** | This is the most common design mistake in small builds. If the site discloses children's personal information to any third party (which includes releasing it to an ad network, but **not** giving it to a provider of support for the internal operations), the cheap email-plus method is unavailable. The site must use one of methods (i) through (vii). Budget for it at proposal time. |
| **Neutral age screen, mixed audience only (312.2)** | For a mixed audience property: collect **no** personal information from **any** visitor, except for the limited 312.5(c) purposes, until age has been collected or another reasonable means of determining whether the visitor is a child has run. "Any collection of age information, or other means of determining whether a visitor is a child, must be done in a **neutral manner that does not default to a set age or encourage visitors to falsify age information**." Build: a blank date-of-birth entry or an unweighted age selection, no prefilled value, no "must be 13+ to continue" banner above it, no yes/no "are you over 13" toggle, no design nudge toward the older answer. Per the 2025 preamble, the neutrality requirement exists so the age screen does not "guide visitors to a particular age or encourage them to indicate they are over the age of 12 through design choices, nudges, communications or site content, or in other ways." Do not add an age screen to a **primarily** child-directed site: the Commission's position is that such a site should assume its audience is under 13. |
| **Block third-party scripts and SDKs before consent** | On a child-directed property, and on a mixed audience property for any visitor identified as under 13, no analytics tag, ad tag, social embed, session recorder, heatmap, chat widget, A/B tool, font loader that logs IPs, or CDN with tracking may fire before verifiable parental consent. Every one of them reads an IP address at minimum, which is personal information under category 7. Implement as an explicit allowlist, not a blocklist: nothing loads unless it is on the list. |
| **The persistent identifier exception (312.5(c)(7)) and its limits** | An operator may collect **a persistent identifier and no other personal information** without consent where it is used "for the sole purpose of providing support for the internal operations of the website or online service," and must then give the 312.4(d)(3) notice. "Support for the internal operations" is defined in 312.2 as activities necessary to: maintain or analyze the functioning of the service; perform network communications; authenticate users or personalize content; **serve contextual advertising or cap the frequency of advertising**; protect security or integrity; ensure legal or regulatory compliance; or fulfill a child's request under 312.5(c)(3) and (4). The proviso is the hard limit: information collected for those activities "cannot be used or disclosed to contact a specific individual, including through behavioral advertising, to amass a profile on a specific individual, or for any other purpose." Practical translation: **contextual ads and frequency capping are permitted without consent; behavioral or interest-based advertising, remarketing, audience building, lookalike modeling, and cross-site profiling are not.** |
| **Ad SDK and tag configuration** | Where advertising runs on a kid-directed property under the contextual-only theory, the ad request must be tagged so the network disables interest-based ads and remarketing. For Google inventory, Google Ad Manager states that "the tag for under age of consent (TFUA) and the tag for child-directed treatment (TFCD) are now deprecated. Instead, use the Tag for age treatment (TFAT)," whose child value 1 is "functionally equivalent to the TFCD or TFUA child treatment tags." Tagless requests take `tfat=1` in the URL. Google's guidance is "You must specify the parameter early in the tag. We recommend that you put it in the first 500 characters". The early placement is a Google recommendation, not a hard cutoff, so treat the 500-character mark as the target rather than a pass/fail line. GPT passbacks use the `TFAT=1` macro. Mobile apps set the age-treatment value through the Google Mobile Ads SDK. **UNVERIFIED:** that legacy TFCD and TFUA continue to function through 2026 with removal in a major release in the first half of 2027. The Ad Manager help page and the GPT privacy samples page both state only that TFCD and TFUA are deprecated; neither publishes a removal date, and the AdMob GDPR page does not either. Migrate to TFAT now regardless, but do not quote a sunset date to a client without re-checking Google's release notes. Tagging is a mitigation, not a substitute for parental consent, and it does not make a non-contextual disclosure lawful. |
| **Parent review and deletion (312.6)** | On a parent's request the operator must provide: a description of the specific types or categories of personal information collected from children; the opportunity at any time to refuse further use or future collection and to direct deletion; and a means of reviewing the information collected. The review mechanism must "ensure that the requestor is a parent of that child, taking into account available technology" and must "not be unduly burdensome to the parent." Build: a documented, staffed request path with an identity check, plus an export of the child's record. The operator may terminate service to a child whose parent refuses further collection (312.6(c)), subject to 312.7. |
| **No conditioning participation (312.7)** | The operator "is prohibited from conditioning a child's participation in a game, the offering of a prize, or another activity on the child's disclosing more personal information than is reasonably necessary to participate in such activity." Build: strip every optional field from kid-facing contest, signup, and game-registration forms. A sweepstakes entry does not need a home address until there is a prize to ship. |
| **Written information security program (312.8(b))** | Beyond "reasonable procedures," the operator "must establish, implement, and maintain a **written** information security program" with safeguards appropriate to the sensitivity of the data and the operator's size, complexity, and nature and scope of activities. Five mandatory elements: (1) designate one or more employees to coordinate it; (2) identify internal and external risks and perform additional assessments **at least annually**; (3) design, implement, and maintain safeguards to control identified risks, each based on volume and sensitivity of data at risk and likelihood of compromise; (4) regularly test and monitor safeguard effectiveness; (5) **at least annually** evaluate and modify the program in light of risks, test results, new methods, or anything with a material impact. This is a deliverable document plus a recurring calendar obligation, not a code change. Say so in the SOW. |
| **Vendor due diligence and written assurances (312.8(c))** | Before allowing other operators, service providers, or third parties to collect or maintain children's personal information on the operator's behalf, or before releasing children's data to them, the operator "must take reasonable steps to determine that such entities are capable of maintaining the confidentiality, security, and integrity of the information and **must obtain written assurances** that such entities will employ reasonable measures." Build: a signed DPA or equivalent for every vendor in the kid-directed data path, collected before launch. Clickwrap terms alone are thin evidence. |
| **Written data retention policy, published (312.10)** | Children's personal information may be retained "only as long as is reasonably necessary to fulfill the specific purpose(s) for which the information was collected," must be deleted with "reasonable measures to protect against unauthorized access to, or use of, the information in connection with its deletion," and **"may not be retained indefinitely."** The operator "must establish, implement, and maintain a written data retention policy that sets forth the purposes for which children's personal information is collected, the business need for retaining such information, and a timeframe for deletion." Critically, "the operator must **provide** its written data retention policy addressing personal information collected from children **in the notice** on the website or online service provided in accordance with 312.4(d)." The policy text goes **inside** the privacy notice. A link to a separate retention page does not satisfy this. "Delete" is defined in 312.2 as removing the information "such that it is not maintained in retrievable form and cannot be retrieved in the normal course of business," which reaches backups and warehouse copies. |
| **Deletion under the consent exceptions** | Several 312.5(c) exceptions carry their own hard deletion duties that must be coded, not documented: contact information collected only to seek consent must be deleted if consent is not obtained "after a reasonable time" (312.5(c)(1)); one-time-response contact information must be deleted "promptly after responding" (312.5(c)(3)); an audio file containing a child's voice collected under 312.5(c)(9) must be deleted "immediately after responding to the child's request." Write these as scheduled jobs with logs. |
| **Records (studio practice, not an express Rule requirement)** | The Rule sets no explicit consent-log retention period for ordinary operators. The three-year record duty in 312.11(d)(3) applies to safe harbor programs, not to operators. Keep, per studio default: the consent record (method used, timestamp, parent identifier, exactly which disclosures were consented to, and the notice version shown), the direct-notice version history, deletion job logs, vendor written assurances, and the annual security-program assessments. The operator carries the burden of showing consent was obtained, so an unlogged consent is functionally no consent. Suggest 24 months past the end of the child's account, subject to the 312.10 duty not to retain the underlying child data longer than needed. |

## How to verify

Run all of these before delivery, on the built site, not on the design.

1. **Confirm the classification in writing.** Get the client to state whether the property is child-directed, mixed audience, or general audience, and get their marketing materials, media kit, app store listing, and ad-targeting parameters. Under the 2025 definition those documents are evidence. If the media kit says "reaching kids 6 to 12" and the site is being built as general audience, stop and escalate.

2. **Cold-load third-party request audit.** Open a fresh browser profile with no cookies, DevTools Network panel, "Disable cache" on, filter to `Fetch/XHR`, `JS`, and `Img`. Load every kid-directed page including the deepest form page. Sort by Domain. Every third-party host must be justified against the 312.2 "support for the internal operations" list. Watch specifically for: `google-analytics.com`, `googletagmanager.com`, `analytics.google.com`, `doubleclick.net`, `googlesyndication.com`, `connect.facebook.net`, `analytics.tiktok.com`, `snap.licdn.com`, `static.hotjar.com`, `cdn.segment.com`, `clarity.ms`, `youtube.com/embed` (use `youtube-nocookie.com` and confirm it still does not set an ID), and any font, map, or captcha CDN. Any of them firing pre-consent on a child-directed page is a finding.

3. **Storage audit.** DevTools > Application > Storage. Check Cookies, Local Storage, Session Storage, and IndexedDB after the cold load and before any consent. Any persistent value that survives a reload and could recognize the user across visits is a persistent identifier under category 7. Then use "Clear site data" and repeat to confirm the result is reproducible.

4. **Grep the repository.** Search the source and the built output for: `gtag(`, `googletagmanager`, `dataLayer`, `fbq(`, `ttq.`, `hotjar`, `clarity`, `segment`, `mixpanel`, `amplitude`, `AdMob`, `admob`, `tagForChildDirectedTreatment`, `tagForUnderAgeOfConsent`, `setPrivacySettings`, `pixel`. Confirm every hit is either removed, gated behind the consent state, or configured for child treatment. Check the built bundle, not only the source, since tag managers inject at runtime.

5. **Ad request tagging.** If ads run, trigger an ad call and inspect the outgoing request in the Network panel. Confirm `tfat=1` (or the current equivalent per Google's Ad Manager documentation) is present, and that it appears early in a tagless request URL, within the first 500 characters per Google's recommendation. Presence is the pass/fail condition; position is a best-practice check. Confirm the returned creative is not interest-based by loading from two profiles with different browsing histories and checking that the creative does not differ by history.

6. **Age screen neutrality (mixed audience).** Load the screen in a fresh profile. Confirm: no personal information request appears before it; no value is prefilled; the control does not default to an adult age; no copy anywhere on the screen states or implies a minimum age; there is no yes/no "are you 13 or older" phrasing; and re-entry after a failed (under 13) answer does not let the user simply resubmit an older age. Then enter an under-13 date and confirm the session is put on the COPPA path with all third-party scripts still blocked.

7. **Notice content check.** Open the children's privacy notice and confirm by reading, item by item, that all five 312.4(d) elements are present. Specifically confirm the **data retention policy text is inline in that page**, with purposes, business need, and a deletion timeframe, and is not merely linked. Confirm named third parties or specific categories appear with their purposes. Confirm every operator is named with address, telephone, and email.

8. **Link placement check.** Confirm the notice link is on the home or landing page or screen, and that it also appears adjacent to every control that collects personal information on a child-facing screen. Walk each form and look for the link within the same visual block, not only in the footer.

9. **Consent flow end to end.** Run the full parent flow with a real address or number. Confirm: the direct notice contains all seven 312.4(c)(1) elements; the two consent choices (collection and use, then third-party disclosure) are separately toggleable; declining the second choice still delivers the core service; nothing is written to any datastore before consent completes; and a consent record is written with method, timestamp, and the exact scopes granted. Then confirm the abandonment path deletes the collected contact information after the configured reasonable time.

10. **Deletion jobs.** Verify the retention timeframe stated in the published policy is actually implemented. Trigger or fast-forward the job and confirm records leave the primary store, the search index, the analytics warehouse, and the backup rotation. A policy that promises 12 months against a database with no deletion job is a false statement in a published notice as well as a 312.10 violation.

11. **Documents exist.** Confirm the client actually holds the written information security program with all five 312.8(b) elements, the dated most recent annual risk assessment, and signed written assurances from every vendor in the children's data path. These are the items most often promised and never produced.

## Penalties

- **Civil penalty per violation: $53,088.** Set by 16 CFR 1.98 as adjusted for inflation effective January 17, 2025 (90 FR 5581), applying "only to penalties assessed after January 17, 2025, including those penalties whose associated violation predated January 17, 2025." A COPPA Rule violation is treated as a violation of a rule defining an unfair or deceptive act or practice under FTC Act Section 18(a)(1)(B) (16 CFR 312.9), so the Section 5(m)(1)(A) penalty applies. Penalties can run per violation per day, and courts have treated each affected child as a separate violation, which is why totals reach nine figures.
- **UNVERIFIED:** whether a 2026 inflation adjustment issued. Partial corroboration: the current text of 16 CFR 1.98 as served on 2026-07-20 still shows $53,088 for Sections 5(l) and 5(m)(1)(A) with the most recent adjustment cited as 90 FR 5581, January 17, 2025, and lists no later adjustment. Secondary reporting attributes the gap to the October 2025 CPI-U not being produced during the appropriations lapse from October 1 to November 12, 2025. No 2026 Federal Register notice was located in this pass, but absence from a mirror is not proof none issued. Re-check 16 CFR 1.98 before quoting the number to a client.
- **Benchmarks actually paid:**
  - *United States v. Epic Games* (December 2022): **$275 million** COPPA civil penalty, described by the FTC as the largest penalty ever obtained for violating an FTC rule, part of $520 million total relief.
  - *United States v. Google and YouTube* (September 4, 2019): **$170 million** total. The $136 million / $34 million split between the FTC and the New York Attorney General is widely and consistently reported but was not read from a primary source in this pass (ftc.gov returned 403). The $170 million total is confirmed.
  - *United States v. Disney* (order approved by the court December 31, 2025): **$10 million** civil penalty, for failing to label some uploaded videos as "Made for Kids" on YouTube, which allowed data collection from child viewers without notice or consent. Penalty amount and approval date confirmed via Hunton. **UNVERIFIED:** the September 2025 complaint date and the "between 2020 and 2022" conduct window; neither was confirmed from the FTC complaint or press release in this pass. Do not quote the date range to a client without pulling the complaint.
  - *Apitor Technology* (2025): **$500,000** civil penalty, suspended on inability to pay, over an app that let a third party in China collect children's geolocation without parental consent. UNVERIFIED: not confirmed from any retrievable source in this pass; ftc.gov refused retrieval and the case does not appear in the secondary sources consulted.
- Beyond money, orders routinely impose deletion of unlawfully collected data and any models or algorithms derived from it, plus multi-year compliance reporting. Assume any COPPA order requires destroying the dataset the client considers valuable.

## Common ways sites fail this

- Google Analytics, Google Tag Manager, or a Meta pixel loading on a kid-directed page. This is the single most common failure. The client asks for analytics, nobody flags that an IP address is personal information under 312.2, and the tag ships.
- An age gate phrased "Are you 13 or older?" with a yes/no toggle, or a date-of-birth field prefilled to a plausible adult year. Both fail the neutrality requirement outright.
- Adding an age screen to a site that is primarily directed to children. It gains nothing (paragraph (3) relief is for mixed audience properties only) and creates a record that the operator knows some users are under 13.
- Using email-plus consent on a site that runs third-party ad tech. Methods (viii) and (ix) are available only to operators that do not disclose children's personal information.
- One combined consent checkbox covering both collection and third-party disclosure. 312.5(a)(2) requires the disclosure consent to be separate and separately refusable.
- Retention policy published as a link from the privacy notice instead of written into it. 312.10 requires the policy be provided in the 312.4(d) notice.
- A retention policy with a stated timeframe and no deletion job behind it.
- Treating the whole site as general audience when one subdirectory (kids' games, a character hub, a school section) is child-directed. The definition says "or portion thereof."
- Embedded YouTube, Instagram, or TikTok players on a kids' page. Standard embeds set identifiers on load.
- A newsletter signup, contest entry, or "email this to a friend" widget on a kid-facing page, collecting email with no consent flow at all.
- Over-collecting on a contest or game registration form (full address, phone, school) when far less is reasonably necessary to participate, contrary to 312.7.
- Mislabeling video uploads on a platform, which is what cost Disney $10 million. If the client publishes to YouTube, the "Made for Kids" designation is part of the compliance surface even though it lives outside the site build.
- Naming no third parties, or writing "we may share with trusted partners." 312.4(d)(2) requires identities or specific categories plus purposes.
- Shipping without the written information security program and vendor written assurances, on the assumption that "reasonable procedures" means whatever the hosting provider does.
- Building an embeddable widget or analytics product, knowing a customer is kid-directed, and assuming the customer carries all the liability. Paragraph (2) of the directed-to-children definition puts the vendor in scope too.

## Notes

**The February 25, 2026 age-verification policy statement is narrow, temporary, and easy to over-read.** The FTC said it will not bring a COPPA enforcement action against "Relevant Operators," meaning **general audience and mixed audience** operators, that collect personal information **solely** to determine a user's age, provided the operator: limits use and disclosure to age determination; retains the data only as long as necessary and promptly deletes it; discloses only to parties that maintain confidentiality and security; gives clear notice to parents and children about what is collected; implements reasonable security safeguards; and takes reasonable steps to ensure the method produces reasonably accurate age determinations. Three limits matter for the build. First, it **does not apply to services primarily directed to children**, which should assume their audience is under 13 rather than age-screen. Second, the discretion evaporates unless the operator is "complying with the COPPA Rule's requirements in every other respect." Third, it binds only the FTC: it does not amend the statute or the Rule, and it does not stop a state attorney general. Firm coverage also reports it does not permit building datasets to train age-estimation algorithms or retaining age data to prevent future circumvention. It remains in effect until the FTC publishes final rule amendments on the issue or withdraws it, so re-check its status at each project.

**A further COPPA rulemaking is signalled.** The Commission has indicated it intends to open a formal Rule review on age verification. Treat the current text as stable through at least the next review cycle, but re-read this file's Status row before quoting deadlines to a client.

**Conflicts and overlaps with other files in this library.**
- *California Eraser Button (Bus. & Prof. Code 22580-22582)*: covers minors **under 18** in California and requires content removal plus specific disclaimers. Entirely separate duties. A COPPA-compliant site can still fail it.
- *State comprehensive privacy laws*: several require opt-in consent for sale or targeted advertising involving consumers under 16, which extends past COPPA's thirteenth-birthday cutoff. Build the under-13 path to COPPA and check whether a 13 to 16 path is separately owed.
- *State age-appropriate design codes*: impose design and default-setting duties beyond COPPA and have been subject to constitutional challenge. Check the current status of each before relying on it.
- **UNVERIFIED: COPPA preemption of inconsistent state law.** 15 U.S.C. 6502(d) is generally described as preempting inconsistent state treatment of the activities COPPA covers. The scope is contested and courts have read it narrowly. Do not tell a client that COPPA compliance preempts a state children's privacy obligation without counsel.

**Third-party ad tech is where these builds die.** The realistic decision for a child-directed property is: no third-party advertising at all, or contextual-only with the age-treatment tag set and a written contract confirming the network will not use the data for profiling. Anything described as "personalized," "interest-based," "remarketing," "audience," or "lookalike" requires separate verifiable parental consent under 312.5(a)(2), and no consent flow that survives contact with actual parents will produce enough opt-ins to make the inventory worth selling. Price the project accordingly and have the advertising conversation at intake, not at launch.

# US State Privacy: comprehensive consumer privacy laws in US states other than California

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| **Jurisdiction** | 23 US states other than California. Each law reaches any business worldwide that either conducts business in the state or targets products or services to residents of that state, and that meets the state's threshold. The visitor's residence is what matters, not where the business or the server sits. |
| **Regulator / enforcer** | State Attorney General in every state in this file. Colorado also allows district attorneys. No state in this file has a private right of action for the comprehensive privacy law itself. Colorado is the only one with a standing rulemaking body (Colorado Department of Law). |
| **In force since** | January 1, 2023 (Virginia CDPA, the first of this family). The template spread from there. |
| **Last amended** | Ongoing. Most recent material change in force: Connecticut SB 1295, effective July 1, 2026, which removed Connecticut's numeric threshold for businesses that sell personal data or process sensitive data. Most recent enactment: Vermont VDPOSA, signed June 16, 2026, effective January 1, 2028. |
| **Last reviewed** | 2026-07-20 |
| **Status** | Partly in force. 19 of the 23 laws are operative as of 2026-07-20. Oklahoma and Louisiana take effect January 1, 2027. Alabama takes effect May 1, 2027. Vermont takes effect January 1, 2028. |

> **This file goes stale faster than anything else in this library.** Four states enacted new laws in the first half of 2026 alone. See the "Update procedure" section at the bottom before you rely on any date or number here.

**Note on the count.** Different trackers say 23 or 24 states. The gap is Florida: the IAPP counts Vermont as the 23rd state and does not treat Florida's FDBR as a comprehensive law because of its $1B platform-scoped applicability. Counting Florida and California, the total is 24. This file lists 23, meaning everything except California.

**Note on New Jersey's date.** The NJDPA was signed January 16, 2024 with a one-year delay. Sources give the effective date as both January 15 and January 16, 2025. The one-day difference has no practical build consequence.

**Note on Connecticut and Delaware signal dates.** Sources conflict. The Connecticut statute text sets January 1, 2025 for opt-out preference signals and the Delaware statute text sets January 1, 2026, but at least one 2026 law-firm summary reverses this. Both dates are now in the past, so the practical answer is the same: honor the signal in both states today. Do not rely on this row to argue a grace period.

## Applies when

Answer these at intake. This section decides whether the client is in scope at all, which is the expensive question.

### Step 1: does the client have US traffic outside California?

- [ ] The site is reachable from the US and is not geo-blocked to California only. If yes, assume every state in the table above is potentially live. There is no "we only operate in one state" defense when the site sells or ships nationally.
- [ ] The business "conducts business in" the state, or "targets products or services to" residents of the state. Shipping there, advertising there, or accepting orders from there all count. Passive reachability alone is the weakest case, but do not build a compliance position on it.

### Step 2: does the client cross a numeric threshold anywhere?

Count **per state**, per calendar year, in consumers (state residents acting in an individual or household capacity). Employees and B2B contacts are generally excluded in this family of laws, unlike California.

- [ ] 100,000 residents of any one state. This is the common ceiling. A regional business almost never hits it. A national ecommerce or SaaS brand often does.
- [ ] 35,000 residents of Delaware, New Hampshire, Maryland, Rhode Island, Connecticut, or Vermont. Much easier to hit. Maryland at 35,000 residents is roughly 0.6% of the state population.
- [ ] 25,000 residents of Montana or Alabama.
- [ ] Any revenue-linked trigger. Several states drop the count sharply if the business sells personal data: 25,000 plus a revenue percentage in most, 10,000 plus 20% in Delaware, Maryland, and Rhode Island, and 15,000 plus 25% in Montana.

### Step 3: the three traps that catch small sites with no threshold

These are the ones that catch a studio's typical client. Read them carefully.

- [ ] **Texas and Nebraska have no numeric threshold.** If the client is not an SBA-defined small business, it is in scope on day one with zero Texas or Nebraska visitors required beyond "targets Texas residents." Even an SBA small business must get consent before selling sensitive data. Check the client against the SBA size standard for its NAICS code rather than guessing.
- [ ] **Connecticut removed its threshold on July 1, 2026** for any business that sells personal data or processes sensitive data. If a client site drops a Meta Pixel and Connecticut visitors reach it, the numeric threshold no longer protects them.
- [ ] **Alabama's 25,000 threshold and its 25% revenue trigger** apply from May 1, 2027 with no consumer count needed on the revenue prong.

### Step 4: is "sale" happening? This is usually the decisive question.

Most of these laws define "sale" as disclosing personal data to a third party for monetary **or other valuable consideration**. Utah, Iowa, and a few others use a narrower monetary-only definition, but do not plan around that.

- [ ] Does the site load Meta Pixel, TikTok Pixel, Google Ads conversion or remarketing tags, LinkedIn Insight, or any ad-network tag? Under the broad definition this is very likely a "sale" or "share," even with no money changing hands and no cookie banner in sight. This single question converts more brochure sites into in-scope controllers than any threshold.
- [ ] Does the site run any cross-context behavioral advertising? That is "targeted advertising" and carries its own opt-out duty separate from sale.
- [ ] Does the site collect anything in the sensitive category? Race or ethnicity, religion, health condition or diagnosis, sexual orientation or sex life, transgender or nonbinary status, immigration or citizenship status, national origin, genetic or biometric data, precise geolocation (generally a radius of 1,750 feet or less), and data from a known child. Connecticut added neural data and certain financial and government ID data effective July 1, 2026.

### Who is NOT caught

- Entity-level exemptions in most states cover HIPAA-covered entities, GLBA-regulated financial institutions, nonprofits, and higher-education institutions. Coverage varies by state and is shrinking: Connecticut removed its financial-institution exemption. Do not assume a client's exemption survives.
- Data-level exemptions cover HIPAA protected health information, FCRA data, GLBA data, FERPA records, and employment and B2B contact data in most of these states.
- Businesses below every threshold in every state, which for a genuinely local single-location client with no ad pixels is a realistic outcome. Document the reasoning in the project file rather than leaving it implicit.

## What the site must have

The common core below applies in essentially every state in the table. The variations section after it lists what changes state by state.

### Common core

| Requirement | What that means in the build |
|---|---|
| Privacy notice | One reachable page, linked from the global footer on every page including checkout and any standalone landing page. It must list the categories of personal data processed, the purpose of each, the categories shared with third parties, the categories of those third parties, how to exercise each right, and how to appeal a denial. A generic template with no actual data-flow content is the most common failure. |
| Sale and targeted-advertising disclosure | If the site sells personal data or runs targeted advertising, the notice must say so in plain language and describe how to opt out. Several states require this statement to be "clear and conspicuous," not buried. |
| Opt-out mechanism, reachable in two clicks | A link or control that turns off sale and targeted advertising. Most states require it to be available from the homepage or an equally accessible location. Build it as a real control, not an email address. |
| Consumer rights request path | Access, correction, deletion, and portability. Two methods where a state requires it. A web form plus an email address is the safe default. Do not require account creation to submit a request. |
| Response deadline | 45 days from receipt in essentially every state in this table, extendable once by another 45 days with notice to the consumer. Build the ticket workflow around that clock. |
| Appeal process | If a request is denied, the consumer must be able to appeal, and the business must respond to the appeal in writing within a set period (commonly 45 or 60 days). The response must include a way to contact the state AG. Nearly every state in this family requires this and nearly every site omits it. |
| Sensitive data consent | Opt-in consent before processing sensitive data in most states. Consent must be a freely given, specific, informed, unambiguous affirmative act. Pre-ticked boxes and "by using this site you agree" do not qualify. |
| Known-child data | Data from a consumer under 13 is treated as sensitive and requires COPPA-grade verifiable parental consent. Several states extend protection to under 18 (see variations). |
| Universal opt-out signal (13 states enacted, 11 operative on 2026-07-20) | Louisiana and Vermont are the two enacted but not yet operative. The server or client must detect the `Sec-GPC: 1` request header or `navigator.globalPrivacyControl === true` and, on detection, suppress sale and targeted-advertising processing for that visitor without asking. This is a build task, not a policy paragraph. |
| Data protection assessments | A written assessment before targeted advertising, sale of personal data, sensitive data processing, certain profiling, and any processing presenting a heightened risk of harm. Required in every state in the table except Utah, Iowa, and Alabama. The AG can demand it during an investigation. All three of the January 2026 laws confirm that power. |
| No dark patterns | Consent obtained through a deceptive interface is not consent, and this is stated in the statutes. Equal visual weight for accept and reject. No pre-checked opt-ins. No "Reject" hidden a layer deeper than "Accept." |
| Anti-retaliation | The site may not degrade service, raise prices, or block content because a consumer exercised a right. Loyalty program differentials are permitted in some states if disclosed and tied to the value of the data. |
| Records to keep | Consent records (what was consented to, when, what text was shown, the UI version), opt-out records (including signal-based opt-outs), the rights-request log with timestamps and outcomes, appeal records, and the data protection assessments. Retain assessments at least as long as the processing continues. |

### Where states differ in ways that change the build

| State | What is different |
|---|---|
| **Maryland** | The strictest in the country. Collection is limited to what is "reasonably necessary and proportionate" to the specific product or service the consumer requested, **even with consent**. Sensitive data may be processed only when "strictly necessary." Selling sensitive data is banned outright, with no consent workaround. Selling personal data or using it for targeted advertising is banned for any consumer the business knows or should know is under 18. Consent-based design does not fix a Maryland problem. |
| **Minnesota** | Consumers can ask why a profiling decision came out the way it did, what they could have done differently, review the data used, and force a re-run on corrected data. Also requires a maintained data inventory and a named chief privacy officer. |
| **Minnesota and Oregon** | Consumers can request the identity of the **specific** third parties their data went to, not just the categories. Category-level disclosure fails here. |
| **Rhode Island** | The privacy notice must name all third parties to whom the controller has sold **or may sell** personal data. Forward-looking, named disclosure. No other state requires this. It also applies to any commercial website with Rhode Island customers that collects, stores, and sells personal information, which is a broader hook than the numeric threshold. |
| **Connecticut** | Since July 1, 2026: no numeric threshold if the business sells data or processes sensitive data, an expanded sensitive-data definition including neural data and transgender or nonbinary status, separate consent required for the **sale** of sensitive data, and a "proportionate" standard layered on top of "reasonably necessary." A separate profiling impact-assessment duty starts August 1, 2026. |
| **Montana** | Since October 1, 2025: threshold cut to 25,000, no cure period at all, consent required for targeted advertising, engagement-maximizing design features, and precise location for users under 18, plus privacy-notice accessibility and multilingual requirements. Also bars returning sensitive identifiers (SSN, government ID, financial account, biometric) in an access-request response. |
| **Tennessee** | Unique affirmative defense: a written privacy program that reasonably conforms to the NIST Privacy Framework is a defense to a TIPA claim. Worth building toward for a client above the $25M gate. |
| **Texas and Nebraska** | Statutorily prescribed notice wording. If the business sells sensitive data or biometric data, the notice must carry the specific sentence the statute dictates. Copy the statutory text verbatim. |
| **Utah and Iowa** | Weakest of the family. Sensitive data is notice-plus-opt-out rather than opt-in consent. No data protection assessments. No universal opt-out signal duty. No correction right in Utah's original text. UNVERIFIED: this file previously stated Utah added a correction right effective July 2026; that amendment could not be confirmed against the Utah Code on 2026-07-20. |
| **Alabama** | No universal opt-out signal duty and no data protection assessments, which makes it the lightest of the 2026 wave. Its "sale" definition excludes analytics and controller-directed marketing services, which is narrower than the rest. |
| **Vermont** | UNVERIFIED: bans selling or using for targeted advertising the data of anyone aged 13 to 17 (age range not confirmed against the bill text). Bars selling consumer health data without consent. Restricts geofencing around health care facilities within 1,850 feet for certain purposes. Consumer health data provisions apply with no threshold to any entity doing business in Vermont. |
| **Florida** | Not really a general business law. The $1B revenue plus platform criteria mean it targets large platforms. It does carry additional duties around voice and video surveillance features and government-directed content moderation that have no analog elsewhere. |

## How to verify

Run all of these before delivery. Each one is a real check with a real pass condition.

### 1. GPC signal handling (the most-failed check)

```bash
# Should return your page with ad/analytics behavior suppressed.
curl -sI -H "Sec-GPC: 1" https://client-site.com | head -20
```

In Chrome DevTools:

1. Install a GPC-sending extension, or set the header manually via DevTools Network conditions.
2. Open the **Network** panel, filter by `facebook.com`, `google-analytics`, `googleadservices`, `analytics.tiktok.com`, `doubleclick`.
3. Reload with the signal on. **Pass condition: zero requests to any ad-network or advertising-identity endpoint.** Analytics with no sale or cross-context advertising component may remain, but confirm the vendor contract supports that.
4. In the **Console**, run `navigator.globalPrivacyControl`. It should return `true` when the extension is active, confirming the signal reaches page JS.
5. Confirm the site's own JS reads it. Grep the bundle: `grep -rn "globalPrivacyControl\|Sec-GPC" ./dist ./src`. If nothing matches and no server-side or CMP path handles it, the site is not honoring the signal regardless of what the policy claims.

### 2. Pre-consent script blocking

1. Open the site in a **fresh incognito window** with no prior state.
2. Before touching the banner, check **Application > Cookies** and **Application > Local Storage**. Advertising and cross-context tracking identifiers must not be present yet in states requiring consent for the underlying processing.
3. Check **Network** for ad-tag requests fired before any interaction.
4. Click **Reject** (not Accept) and reload. Pass condition: the rejected categories stay off across the reload, which means the choice persisted.

### 3. Footer and link reachability

```bash
# Every page should carry the privacy link. Check the rendered output, not the template.
grep -rion "privacy[- ]policy\|do not sell\|your privacy choices" ./dist/**/*.html | sort -u
```

Click through from the homepage: privacy notice must be reachable in one click, opt-out control in no more than two. Check checkout pages and standalone campaign landing pages specifically, since those are where the global footer is most often dropped.

### 4. Rights request path

1. Submit a real access request through the live form as an anonymous member of the public.
2. Confirm it lands somewhere a human monitors. A form posting to a dead inbox is the single most common delivery defect.
3. Confirm the 45-day clock is tracked somewhere, whether a ticket system, a spreadsheet, or a calendar reminder. Hand the client the process in writing.
4. Confirm an appeal path exists and that the denial template names the state AG contact route.

### 5. Notice content audit

Read the deployed privacy notice against the actual site. For each script in the page source, confirm the notice discloses that category of sharing. Grep the page for every third-party domain:

```bash
grep -roh 'https\?://[a-z0-9.-]*\.[a-z]\{2,\}' ./dist | sed 's|https\?://||' | cut -d/ -f1 | sort -u
```

Every third-party domain in that output should be traceable to a disclosed category in the notice. For a Rhode Island-exposed client, third parties that receive sold data must be named individually.

### 6. Consent record spot check

Trigger a consent event and confirm a record is written containing the timestamp, the choice, the categories, and an identifier for the exact banner text and version shown. A boolean `consent=true` with no version reference is not defensible evidence.

### 7. Accessibility of the consent UI

Tab through the banner with the keyboard only. Reject must be reachable and operable without a mouse, and must carry the same visual weight as Accept. A reject control that is keyboard-unreachable is both a dark pattern and an ADA and WCAG problem. See `ada-wcag.md` in this library.

Per violation, and "violation" is commonly read as per consumer per incident, which is what makes these numbers matter.

| Florida | Up to $50,000 per violation, subject to trebling in certain cases | Discretionary 45 days, no sunset |
| Maryland | $10,000 first violation, $25,000 subsequent | 60 days, sunsets April 1, 2027 |

| New Hampshire | $10,000 per violation | Expired January 1, 2026 |
| Rhode Island | Two tracks. The RIDTPPA's own penalty is $100 to $500 for each intentional disclosure (R.I. Gen. Laws 6-48.1-8). The AG may instead enforce under the state deceptive trade practices act, which carries up to $10,000 per violation | None, enforceable immediately |
| Tennessee | $7,500 per violation, trebled for willful or knowing violations | 60 days |

**Direction of travel: cure periods are disappearing.** Colorado, Connecticut, Delaware, New Hampshire, Minnesota, Oregon, and Montana have all lost theirs. Rhode Island never had one. A build shipped in 2026 with an obvious defect no longer has a free first strike in most of the country.

## Common ways sites fail this

1. **Ad pixels fire before the consent decision, or regardless of it.** The banner appears, the user clicks Reject, and the Meta Pixel is already in the network log. This is the number one finding and it is visible in DevTools in ten seconds.
2. **GPC is ignored.** The privacy policy says the site honors opt-out preference signals. Nothing in the codebase reads `Sec-GPC` or `navigator.globalPrivacyControl`. This is a written admission attached to a verifiable failure, which is exactly the pattern regulators pursue.
3. **A California-only template on a national site.** The policy addresses CCPA rights, says nothing about the other 23 states, and lists no appeal process. The appeal right is nearly universal in this family and nearly universally missing.
4. **The "Do Not Sell" link exists but does nothing.** It opens a modal, the toggle flips, and the tag manager never receives the change. Verify the network behavior after the toggle, never the toggle state.
5. **Threshold reasoning is never written down.** The client is told "you're too small to worry about it" with no record of who checked, against which state, on what date. Texas and Nebraska have no numeric threshold, so this reasoning is frequently wrong on its face.
6. **The rights request form posts into a void.** Nobody owns the inbox, the 45-day clock is untracked, and the first real request is discovered during an AG inquiry.
7. **Sensitive data is collected through an ordinary form field.** A health intake, a dietary restriction field, a "tell us about your accessibility needs" box, or a precise geolocation prompt collects sensitive data with no separate opt-in consent and no assessment.
8. **The privacy notice names the wrong entity or the wrong state.** Copied from another client, still carrying their business name or their state. Grep every deployed policy for the previous client's name before delivery.
9. **Reject is styled as a ghost link next to a solid Accept button.** A dark pattern under these statutes, and consent obtained through it is not valid consent.
10. **Consent is logged as a bare boolean.** No banner version, no timestamp, no record of what text was shown. Useless as evidence.

## Update procedure

This file describes 23 separate statutes, each amendable every legislative session, in a subject area that added four states in six months. Treat any number here as stale after six months.

**Review cadence: every six months, and additionally in June and July each year** (most state legislative sessions end in spring, so new enactments and amendments cluster in May and June with effective dates the following January).

**On each review, in this order:**

1. Open the [IAPP US State Privacy Legislation Tracker](https://iapp.org/resources/article/us-state-privacy-legislation-tracker) and check its "last updated" date and its state count against the 23 in this file (24 counting California). If the count moved, a state was added.
2. For any new state, capture: short name, bill number, signing date, effective date, thresholds, whether it requires universal opt-out signal recognition, whether it requires data protection assessments, penalty maximum, and cure period. Those nine fields are what the table needs.
3. Check the amendment column. Amendments to existing laws are the more dangerous change because they alter a state already assumed handled. Connecticut in 2026 and Montana in 2025 both removed or gutted a threshold that a prior build relied on.
4. Re-verify every threshold marked UNVERIFIED in this file against a primary source, and remove the marker only when the statute or the state AG confirms it.
5. Update the **Last reviewed** field at the top and note what changed in the Notes section.
6. Commit with the message `legal: refresh us-state-privacy-laws (<what changed>)`.

**Fields in this file that are currently unverified and should be resolved first:**

- Florida FDBR universal opt-out signal duty and data protection assessment duty.
- Rhode Island universal opt-out signal duty (assumed none, not confirmed).
- Montana, Louisiana, and Vermont penalty maximums.
- Whether Vermont's VDPOSA bans sale and targeted advertising for ages 13 to 17. The provision is asserted in this file but was not confirmed against the bill text or a second source on 2026-07-20.
- Whether Utah added a correction right effective July 2026.

## Notes

**Watch these.**

- **Connecticut's threshold removal is the template to watch.** SB 1295 detached applicability from consumer counts for any business that sells data or processes sensitive data. If other states copy it, the "we're too small" position collapses across the country and every client with an ad pixel becomes an in-scope controller. Check for copycat bills each session.
- **Cure periods are ending.** Seven states have already lost theirs. Design and QA for correctness at launch rather than assuming a warning letter arrives first.
- **Universal opt-out signal recognition is the enforcement target of choice.** It is machine-testable at scale, a regulator can automate detection across thousands of sites, and a mismatch between the privacy policy's claim and the site's behavior is easy to prove. Honoring GPC everywhere, in all 50 states, costs almost nothing to implement and removes the highest-probability finding.
- **Minor-directed rules are diverging sharply from the adult rules.** Maryland bans sale and targeted advertising for under-18s regardless of consent, Vermont is reported to ban it for ages 13 to 17 (UNVERIFIED, see the variations table), and Montana requires consent for engagement-maximizing design features aimed at minors. If a client site plausibly reaches teenagers, these rules bind at a level the general privacy notice does not address. Cross-reference `ca-minors-eraser-button.md` in this library, which covers a different California statute with an independent trigger.
- **Overlap with the California file.** A national site is subject to California and to the states here at the same time. The obligations are compatible, but the vocabulary is not: California says "sale" and "sharing" and "limit the use of my sensitive personal information," while these states say "sale" and "targeted advertising" and "sensitive data consent." Write one policy that satisfies the strictest requirement in each category rather than maintaining state-by-state variants, then add the state-specific supplements that Rhode Island, Minnesota, and Oregon require.
- **Washington My Health My Data and Nevada SB 370 are not in this file.** They are consumer health data laws, not comprehensive privacy laws, and Washington's carries a private right of action, which makes it a materially higher-risk statute than anything here. If a client touches health-adjacent data, that is a separate file and a separate conversation.
- **Vermont's consumer health data provisions apply with no threshold**, so Vermont will bind small clients from January 1, 2028 that no other law in this table reaches.
- **The recommended default build** for any US client site: honor GPC nationwide, block advertising and non-essential tags until consent, publish one policy covering the strictest standard across categories, ship a working opt-out control and a monitored rights-request path with an appeal route, and log consent with version references. That configuration satisfies the great majority of the 23 statutes without per-state branching, and it is cheaper than determining exact applicability for each one.

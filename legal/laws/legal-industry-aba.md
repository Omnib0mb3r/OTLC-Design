# Attorney advertising: ABA Model Rules 7.1 to 7.3 and 1.18, as amended in 2018, plus state bar variation

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| **Jurisdiction** | United States, state by state. There is no federal attorney advertising regulator and no single national rule. Each state supreme court adopts its own rules of professional conduct. The obligation attaches to the lawyer, not to the studio and not to the visitor. |
| **Regulator / enforcer** | State supreme courts and the disciplinary bodies they charter (state bars, disciplinary commissions, grievance committees). The ABA itself regulates nobody. Its Model Rules are drafting templates that a state may adopt whole, adopt in part, or ignore. Two states run a mandatory pre-dissemination or post-dissemination filing program for advertising: Florida (The Florida Bar, rule 4-7.19) and Texas (State Bar of Texas Advertising Review Committee, rule 7.04). |
| **In force since** | The ABA Model Rules of Professional Conduct date from 1983. The current structure of the 7-series comes from the 2018 amendments, which consolidated the old Rules 7.2, 7.4, and 7.5 into Rules 7.1 and 7.2. |
| **Last amended** | Model Rules: the ABA table of contents retrieved 2026-07-20 lists "Rule 7.4 (Deleted)" and "Rule 7.5 (Deleted)", and titles Rule 7.2 "Communications Concerning a Lawyer's Services: Specific Rules". UNVERIFIED: the amendments are commonly attributed to a resolution adopted by the ABA House of Delegates in August 2018. The resolution number and adoption date could not be confirmed against a primary ABA document in this pass. State rules move separately and more recently: Florida chapter 4 is current through June 30, 2026, with rule 4-7.20 amended May 15, 2025, effective July 14, 2025 (SC2025-0018); California Business and Professions Code section 6157 was amended effective January 1, 2026 (Stats. 2025, Ch. 645); California Rule 7.3(f) and Comment [5] took effect June 1, 2026. |
| **Last reviewed** | 2026-07-20 |
| **Status** | In force. Stable at the Model Rule level since 2018, actively moving at the state level. Re-check the client's own state before every launch. |

## Applies when

Work through these at intake. The first question decides whether this file applies at all; the second decides how many rulebooks you are building against.

**Is the client a lawyer or a law firm?**

- **Yes, in scope:** any licensed attorney or law firm site, solo through large firm, whether the site sells services or only describes them. Model Rule 7.1 reaches "all communications about a lawyer's services, including advertising" (Comment [1]). There is no brochure-site carve-out.
- **Yes, in scope:** lawyer referral services, qualifying providers, and lawyer directories in states that regulate them by name. Florida rule 4-7.12(a)(1) writes the referral service, qualifying provider, and lawyer directory into the required-content rule alongside the lawyer.
- **Probably in scope even without a local office:** Florida rule 4-7.11(b) applies subchapter 4-7 to lawyers "whether or not admitted to practice in Florida or other jurisdictions, who advertise that the lawyer provides legal services in Florida or who target advertisements for legal employment at Florida residents." Targeting is the trigger, not admission.
- **Not in scope here:** non-lawyer businesses in legal-adjacent work (document preparers, legal tech vendors, title companies, paralegal services). They fall to FTC advertising law and to state unauthorized-practice-of-law statutes instead. See the FTC file. Do not assume they are unregulated.
- **Mixed sites need a split analysis.** Florida's comment to rule 4-7.11 says subchapter 4-7 applies to the portions of a multistate firm's website that relate to legal services in Florida or by a Florida Bar member, and does not apply to portions relating to services in other jurisdictions by lawyers not admitted in Florida. A site can be partly regulated by a given state and partly not, page by page.

**Which states' rules apply?**

Every state where the firm practices, advertises, or targets. For a website that is every state the firm holds itself out in, which for most firm sites is more than one.

- **The formal rule is Model Rule 8.5, not a "strictest state wins" rule.** Get this right, because the shorthand is wrong on the law. Rule 8.5(a) subjects a lawyer to the disciplinary authority of a jurisdiction where the lawyer is admitted "regardless of where the lawyer's conduct occurs", and also to a jurisdiction where the lawyer is not admitted "if the lawyer provides or offers to provide any legal services in this jurisdiction." Rule 8.5(b)(2) then picks which rulebook applies: "the rules of the jurisdiction in which the lawyer's conduct occurred, or, if the predominant effect of the conduct is in a different jurisdiction, the rules of that jurisdiction shall be applied to the conduct." It adds a safe harbor: "A lawyer shall not be subject to discipline if the lawyer's conduct conforms to the rules of a jurisdiction in which the lawyer reasonably believes the predominant effect of the lawyer's conduct will occur."
- **Build to the strictest applicable state anyway.** That is a design decision, not a legal conclusion. Predominant effect is a fact question that gets resolved after a complaint, which is exactly when you do not want to be litigating it. Building to the strictest state costs a disclaimer and a retention job. Guessing wrong costs the client a grievance file. Say it to the client in those terms and let their counsel own the call.
- **Some states are materially stricter than the Model Rules.** Florida, Texas, and New York each impose requirements the Model Rules do not contain. California keeps Rules 7.4 and 7.5 that the ABA deleted, and layers a statute (Business and Professions Code article 9.5) on top of the conduct rules. Build against those four specifically if the firm touches them.

**Who is on the hook?**

The lawyer, always. The studio is not subject to bar discipline. That does not make the studio safe: the studio ships the violation, the client absorbs the grievance, and the engagement ends badly. Two rules make the relationship explicit. Model Rule 7.2(d) requires that "Any communication made under this Rule must include the name and contact information of at least one lawyer or law firm responsible for its content." California's comment [3] to Rule 7.2 names the studio directly, permitting a lawyer to compensate "vendors who are engaged to provide marketing or client-development services, such as publicists, public-relations personnel, business-development staff, and website designers", and pointing to Rule 5.3 for the lawyer's duty to supervise nonlawyers who prepare marketing materials. Expect the client's counsel or ethics partner to review the copy. Build the site so that review is cheap.

**Thresholds.** None. There is no firm-size, revenue, or traffic threshold. A solo practitioner's one-page site is fully covered.

## What the site must have

| Requirement | What that means in the build |
|---|---|
| A responsible lawyer named with contact information | Model Rule 7.2(d): every communication must include "the name and contact information of at least one lawyer or law firm responsible for its content." Put the firm's legal name, a street address, and a phone number in the global footer so it renders on every page. Florida rule 4-7.12(a) requires the name of at least one responsible lawyer or the firm, plus "the city, town, or county of 1 or more bona fide office locations of the lawyer who will perform the services advertised." New York rule 7.1(h) requires "the name, principal law office address and telephone number of the lawyer or law firm whose services are being offered." A footer with only a contact form and a city name fails all three. |
| Jurisdictional admission statement | No Model Rule states this as a standalone requirement. It comes out of Rule 7.1, because omitting it is the omission "necessary to make the statement considered as a whole not materially misleading." Florida's comment to rule 4-7.13 gives the exact failure: "An example of the implied existence of a nonexistent fact is an advertisement stating that a lawyer has offices in multiple states if the lawyer is not licensed in those states or is not authorized to practice law." Build it as a per-attorney field on every bio page ("Admitted in: New York, New Jersey") plus a site-level line in the footer or legal notice page. If the firm has an office in a state where nobody is admitted, say so on the office page. |
| No specialist or expert claim without certification, with the certifying body named | Model Rule 7.2(c): "A lawyer shall not state or imply that a lawyer is certified as a specialist in a particular field of law, unless: (1) the lawyer has been certified as a specialist by an organization that has been approved by an appropriate authority of the state or the District of Columbia or a U.S. Territory or that has been accredited by the American Bar Association; and (2) the name of the certifying organization is clearly identified in the communication." Both conditions, not one. Naming the organization is not optional. See the specialization section below for the words that trip this. |
| Past-results disclaimer wherever outcomes appear | Model Rule 7.1 Comment [3] is the hook, and it names the fix: "The inclusion of an appropriate disclaimer or qualifying language may preclude a finding that a statement is likely to create unjustified expectations or otherwise mislead the public." New York rule 7.1(e)(3) hard-codes the wording: the content must be "accompanied by the following disclaimer: 'Prior results do not guarantee a similar outcome'". Render it adjacent to each results block, not once in the footer. |
| Testimonial handling and disclaimers | Florida rule 4-7.13(b)(9) makes a testimonial deceptive if it is not the actual experience of the person, does not represent what clients generally experience, was drafted by the lawyer, was given in exchange for something of value, or "does not include the disclaimer that the prospective client may not obtain the same or similar results." New York rule 7.1(c)(1) bars a paid endorsement or testimonial "without disclosing that the person is being compensated therefor", and rule 7.1(e)(4) requires informed consent confirmed in writing for a testimonial about a matter still pending. See the testimonials section below. |
| "Attorney Advertising" label where the state requires it | New York rule 7.1(f) requires the label "on the first page, or on the home page in the case of a web site", and rule 7.1(i) says that in the case of a web site "the required words or statements shall appear on the home page." Not the footer of an interior page. The home page. California Rule 7.3(c) separately requires the word "Advertisement" or similar at the beginning and ending of a written, recorded, or electronic communication soliciting employment from a person known to need legal services, which reaches targeted email rather than the public site. |
| A "not legal advice, no attorney-client relationship" notice on every entry point | No Model Rule mandates this sentence. Rule 1.18 Comment [2] is what makes it load-bearing, and it is the single most important paragraph in this file for a developer. See the intake section below. Place the notice on the contact page, above every form's submit button, in the chatbot's opening message, and on the disclaimer page linked from the footer. |
| Conflicts checking before free-text intake | Rule 1.18(b) attaches a confidentiality duty to information from a prospective client "[e]ven when no client-lawyer relationship ensues", and 1.18(c) can disqualify the whole firm. The form design decides how much disqualifying information the firm receives. See the intake section. |
| No hidden text or metadata that would violate the rules if visible | New York rule 7.1(g): "A lawyer or law firm shall not utilize meta tags or other hidden computer codes that, if displayed, would violate these Rules." This is a direct instruction to the person writing the templates. It reaches `<meta name="keywords">`, `<title>`, alt text, JSON-LD, `aria-label`, `sr-only` blocks, hidden H1s, and competitor names stuffed into schema markup. |
| Advertising records retained | New York rule 7.1(k) and California Business and Professions Code section 6159.1. Both are covered in the retention section below. This is a build task, not only a client task, because the client cannot snapshot a site they do not control. |
| Filing with the state bar where required | Texas rule 7.04 and Florida rule 4-7.19, each with a website-specific exemption that people routinely get backwards. See the filing section. |
| No fee or outcome promise the firm cannot honor | Texas rule 7.02(d) binds advertised fees "for the period during which the advertisement is reasonably expected to be in circulation" but not longer than one year unless expressly promised. New York rule 7.1(l) says a firm that advertises a range of fees or an hourly rate "shall not charge more than the fee advertised for such services." A stale pricing page is a rule violation, not only bad copy. Date-stamp fee content and put it under CMS control so it can be changed without a deploy. |

### Specialization claims: the words that trip Rule 7.2(c)

The prohibition is on stating **or implying** certification as a specialist. Copy fails on the implication, so the audit is a word-level audit of the whole site.

Words and patterns that trigger it: "specialist", "specializing in", "expert", "expertise", "certified", "board certified", "our specialty", and headline constructions like "Denver's DUI Specialists". Also check the `<title>`, H1s, nav labels, breadcrumb text, image alt text, and URL slugs, since Rule 7.1 governs the communication as a whole and New York rule 7.1(g) reaches hidden codes specifically.

The certification exception has two conditions and both must be satisfied in the visible copy:

1. The lawyer is actually certified by an approved or ABA-accredited organization.
2. The name of the certifying organization is clearly identified in the same communication.

State variants you will hit:

- **Texas** prescribes the wording. Rule 7.02(b) permits a competence claim where the lawyer holds a "Certificate of Special Competence by the Texas Board of Legal Specialization" and the advertisement states "Board Certified, area of specialization -- Texas Board of Legal Specialization", or where the lawyer belongs to an organization accredited by that board using published admission criteria. Copy the required phrasing exactly and let the client supply the area of specialization.
- **Florida** splits certification from specialization and treats both as potentially misleading unless conditions are met. Rule 4-7.14(a)(4) allows "board certified" only where the lawyer is certified under the Florida Certification Plan, or by an ABA-accredited or Florida Bar accredited program, or by another state bar with comparable standards, and in each case "the advertisement includes the area of certification and the name of the certifying organization." Rule 4-7.14(a)(5) separately allows "specialist" or "expert" where the lawyer is certified under one of those plans **or** where "the lawyer can objectively verify the claim based on the lawyer's education, training, experience, or substantial involvement in the area of practice". Rule 4-7.14(a)(6) extends this to firms and adds a disclaimer requirement: if the firm cannot verify the claim for every lawyer it employs, "the advertisement must contain a clear and conspicuous disclaimer that not all lawyers in the firm specialize or have expertise in the area of practice in which the firm claims specialization or expertise." A practice-area landing page that says "Our Estate Planning Specialists" needs that disclaimer on the page.
- **California** kept a dedicated rule. Rule 7.4(a) bars stating that the lawyer "is a certified specialist in a particular field of law" unless currently certified by the Board of Legal Specialization or another State Bar accredited entity, and "the name of the certifying organization is clearly identified in the communication." Rule 7.4(b) is more permissive than most states on the underlying description: "a lawyer may communicate the fact that the lawyer does or does not practice in particular fields of law. A lawyer may also communicate that his or her practice specializes in, is limited to, or is concentrated in a particular field of law, subject to the requirements of rule 7.1." The regulated claim in California is *certified* specialist, not the word specializes standing alone. Do not carry that permission into another state.
- **New York** requires an added disclaimer with the certification, and specifies its type size. Rule 7.4(c)(1) permits stating certification by an ABA-approved private organization only if the organization is identified and this statement "is prominently made: 'The [name of the private certifying organization] is not affiliated with any governmental authority.'" Rule 7.4(c)(3)(i) defines prominent for written media: "clearly legible and capable of being read by the average person, and is in a font size at least two font sizes larger than the largest text used to state the fact of certification." That is a CSS requirement. If the certification badge is set at 18px, the disclaimer cannot be 12px small print.

The safe default when the client is not certified: describe what the firm does rather than what the firm is. "We handle immigration matters" and "Immigration" as a practice-area label are descriptions. "Immigration Law Specialists" is a claim.

### Past results and testimonials

Past results are not banned by the Model Rules. They are governed by Rule 7.1, and Comment [3] explains the mechanism: a communication "that truthfully reports a lawyer's achievements on behalf of clients or former clients may be misleading if presented so as to lead a reasonable person to form an unjustified expectation that the same results could be obtained for other clients in similar matters without reference to the specific factual and legal circumstances of each client's case." The same comment covers comparative claims: an unsubstantiated comparison of a lawyer's services or fees with those of other firms "may be misleading if presented with such specificity as would lead a reasonable person to conclude that the comparison or claim can be substantiated."

Build rules that follow from that:

- **Attach the disclaimer to the results, not to the site.** A results carousel on the home page, a "$4.2M verdict" stat tile, and a case-results archive each need the qualifying language rendered with them. A single line on a legal page nobody visits does not qualify the home page claim.
- **Use the state's exact words where the state supplies them.** New York rule 7.1(e)(3) specifies "Prior results do not guarantee a similar outcome". California Business and Professions Code section 6158.3 requires, for electronic media advertising depicting case results, either a disclosure of "the factual and legal circumstances that justify the result portrayed" or a statement "that the result portrayed in the advertisement was dependent on the facts of that case, and that the results will differ if based on different facts." Section 6157 defines "electronic medium" as "[t]elevision, radio, or computer networks", so a website is inside the definition.
- **Texas has a trap specific to verdict numbers.** Rule 7.01(g) requires that where a lawyer advertises the amount of a verdict and knows the verdict was reduced, reversed, or settled for less, the amount actually received must be disclosed with equal prominence. Equal prominence is a typography requirement. If the headline number is 64px, the corrected number cannot be a footnote. Build the case-result content type with a required "amount actually recovered" field so the CMS cannot publish a bare verdict figure.
- **Florida requires objective verifiability, not just a disclaimer.** Rule 4-7.13(b)(2) makes "references to past results, unless the information is objectively verifiable" deceptive per se, and 4-7.13(b)(3) does the same for comparisons and for "statements, words, or phrases that characterize a lawyer's or law firm's skills, experience, reputation, or record, unless the characterization is objectively verifiable." "Aggressive representation" and "the best trial lawyers in Tampa" are the ordinary casualties.
- **Watch the combined-experience claim.** Florida's comment to rule 4-7.13 names it: "An example of a material omission is stating 'over 20 years' experience' when the experience is the combined experience of all lawyers in the advertising firm." Also flagged is counting time in nonlawyer roles toward a years-of-experience figure. A stat band reading "75+ Years Combined Experience" needs the word combined in the same visual unit as the number, not below the fold.

For testimonials and reviews, the constraints stack:

- Florida rule 4-7.13(b)(9) requires the testimonial to be the person's actual experience, to be representative of what clients generally experience, to not have been written by the lawyer, to not have been paid for, and to carry "the disclaimer that the prospective client may not obtain the same or similar results". A wall of five-star pull quotes with no disclaimer fails on its face.
- New York rule 7.1(c)(1) requires disclosing compensation for any paid endorsement or testimonial, and rule 7.1(e)(4) requires informed consent confirmed in writing where the client's matter is still pending.
- New York rule 7.1(c)(3) requires disclosure where actors portray a judge, the lawyer, firm members, or clients, or where the material uses "depictions of fictionalized events or scenes". This reaches stock photography presented as the firm's own team or clients. Florida rule 4-7.13(b)(5) is the parallel provision and requires "a clear and conspicuous disclaimer that the person is not an employee or member of the law firm."
- **Embedded review widgets are a live compliance surface.** A Google Reviews or Avvo embed pulls text the firm did not write and cannot pre-screen, into a page the firm is responsible for under Rule 7.2(d). Treat a third-party review embed as a launch decision to escalate to the client's counsel, not a component choice. If it ships, the required disclaimer must render in the page, outside the iframe, where the widget cannot drop it.
- The FTC endorsement rules apply on top of the bar rules. See the FTC disclosures file. Complying with one does not discharge the other.

### Chatbots, intake forms, and Rule 1.18

This is the highest-risk part of a modern law firm build, and it is the part most likely to be handed to a developer with no legal review at all.

**The rule.** Model Rule 1.18(a): "A person who consults with a lawyer about the possibility of forming a client-lawyer relationship with respect to a matter is a prospective client." Rule 1.18(b): "Even when no client-lawyer relationship ensues, a lawyer who has learned information from a prospective client shall not use or reveal that information, except as Rule 1.9 would permit with respect to information of a former client." Rule 1.18(c) extends the consequence to the firm: if the lawyer received information "that could be significantly harmful to that person in the matter", the lawyer is disqualified from representing an adverse party in the same or a substantially related matter, and "no lawyer in a firm with which that lawyer is associated may knowingly undertake or continue representation in such a matter" absent the cures in 1.18(d).

**Why the form design decides the outcome.** Rule 1.18 Comment [2] draws the line, and it draws it around what the site does:

> a consultation is likely to have occurred if a lawyer, either in person or through the lawyer's advertising in any medium, specifically requests or invites the submission of information about a potential representation without clear and reasonably understandable warnings and cautionary statements that limit the lawyer's obligations, and a person provides information in response.

The same comment gives the other side: a consultation does not occur where a person provides information "in response to advertising that merely describes the lawyer's education, experience, areas of practice, and contact information, or provides legal information of general interest." That person "communicates information unilaterally", which New York codifies as an express exclusion at its rule 1.18(e)(1): a person is not a prospective client if the person "communicates information unilaterally to a lawyer, without any reasonable expectation that the lawyer is willing to discuss the possibility of forming a client-lawyer relationship."

So the build controls two variables: how much the interface **invites** case facts, and whether **warnings** are present when it does. A textarea labeled "Tell us about your case" with no warning is the fact pattern Comment [2] describes.

**What to build.**

1. **Put the warning where the information is entered.** Not only on a linked terms page. Render it as visible text immediately above the submit control, and inside the chatbot's first message before the user can type. The client's counsel supplies the wording. Do not draft it. A representative structure the client's counsel can start from: a statement that submitting the form does not create an attorney-client relationship, that the firm has not agreed to represent the sender, that the sender should not send confidential or time-sensitive information until an engagement letter is signed, and that nothing on the site is legal advice. Ship whatever the client's counsel returns, verbatim.
2. **Make the warning unavoidable, not dismissible.** A checkbox the user must tick is stronger evidence than a paragraph they may have scrolled past. Store the acknowledgment with a timestamp and the version of the notice shown, alongside the submission. The firm will want that record if a disqualification motion ever lands.
3. **Version the disclaimer text and keep the history.** When counsel revises the wording, the firm needs to prove which version a given submitter saw. Treat the notice as content with a version identifier, not a hardcoded string.
4. **Collect the minimum, and collect identity before facts.** Rule 1.18 Comment [4] tells the lawyer to "limit the initial consultation to only such information as reasonably appears necessary" to decide whether to take the matter. The build equivalent is a two-step form. Step one takes name, contact details, adverse party name, and matter type, which is what a conflicts check needs. Step two, which opens the free-text field, is reached only after the firm clears conflicts. A single-page form with an open "describe your situation" box collects disqualifying information before anyone has checked whether the firm represents the other side.
5. **Do not let a chatbot ask follow-up questions about the facts.** A retrieval-style bot that answers "where is your office" and "what practice areas do you handle" is closer to the Comment [2] safe side, because it describes the practice. A bot that asks "what happened?", "when did the accident occur?", or "who was the other driver?" is soliciting exactly the information that triggers 1.18(b) and 1.18(c). If the client wants a conversational intake bot, that is a decision for their counsel, in writing, with the warning shown before the first user turn.
6. **Generative chatbots carry an extra failure mode.** A model that answers a visitor's legal question is producing something a reasonable visitor may read as legal advice, and its output is a communication about the lawyer's services under Rule 7.1, for which a named lawyer is responsible under Rule 7.2(d). The firm cannot pre-review text that does not exist until it is generated. Constrain the bot to retrieval over firm-approved content, disable free-form legal answering, log every exchange, and put the record in front of the client. Do not ship an unconstrained model on a law firm site.
7. **Route submissions to a system the firm controls, with access limits.** Rule 1.18(d)(2) contemplates screening the disqualified lawyer from the matter. Screening is impossible if every intake email lands in a shared inbox the whole firm reads. Deliver to a controlled intake queue with per-user access, and turn off the plugin that copies submissions to the studio.
8. **Keep the studio out of the content.** Do not let form submissions reach the studio's inbox, error tracker, or analytics. Prospective-client information carries a confidentiality duty under 1.18(b). Scrub request bodies in Sentry and equivalents, and confirm no session-replay tool is recording keystrokes in the intake fields.

**A note on what 1.18 is not.** Solicitation under Rule 7.3 runs the other direction and mostly does not reach a website. Comment [1] to Model Rule 7.3: "A lawyer's communication is not a solicitation if it is directed to the general public, such as through a billboard, an Internet banner advertisement, a website or a television commercial, or if it is in response to a request for information or is automatically generated in response to electronic searches." Comment [2] adds that "[l]ive person-to-person contact" covering the 7.3(b) prohibition "does not include chat rooms, text messages or other written communications that recipients may easily disregard." Iowa's version of the same comment spells out the list further, excluding "chats, text messages, email, personal messages within social media platforms, or other written communications". A website chat widget is therefore not the Rule 7.3 problem people assume. It is a Rule 1.18 problem. Keep the two straight when you brief the client.

### Advertising records retention

Two states impose retention duties that a developer has to design for, because a firm cannot preserve a site it does not control.

- **New York rule 7.1(k)** is the demanding one. All advertisements must be pre-approved by the lawyer or firm and a copy retained "for a period of not less than three years following its initial dissemination." Advertising in a computer-accessed communication must be retained "for a period of not less than one year." Then the website-specific sentence: "A copy of the contents of any web site covered by this Rule shall be preserved upon the initial publication of the web site, any major web site redesign, or a meaningful and extensive content change, but in no event less frequently than once every 90 days." Build this as a scheduled job, not a manual reminder. A quarterly full-site crawl to WARC or a static mirror, timestamped and stored where the firm can retrieve it, satisfies the cadence. Trigger an extra capture on redesign and on any substantial content change. Hand the client the archive and the runbook at launch, because the duty outlives the engagement.
- **California Business and Professions Code section 6159.1**: "A true and correct copy of any advertisement made by a person or licensee shall be retained for one year by the person or licensee who pays for an advertisement soliciting employment of legal services." Shorter and simpler than New York, and it attaches to whoever pays for the advertisement.
- **Texas** ties retention to its filing regime instead. Rule 7.04 requires filing a copy with the Advertising Review Committee, which produces a record at the bar rather than at the firm.
- **Other states vary.** Check the client's state before promising the client that no archive is needed. Absence of a rule in the four states covered here is not evidence of absence elsewhere.

### Filing and pre-review, where the state requires it

Both mandatory-filing states carve out websites, and both carve them out only partially. This is where people confidently get it wrong in both directions.

- **Texas.** Rule 7.04 requires a lawyer to file with the Advertising Review Committee of the State Bar of Texas a copy of the advertisement, a completed application, and the fee set by the Board of Directors, "no later than ten (10) days after the date of dissemination of an advertisement of legal services, or ten (10) days after the date of a solicitation communication." Filing is after dissemination, not before. Rule 7.05(b) then exempts "information and links posted on a law firm website, except the contents of the website homepage." Read that carefully: the interior pages are exempt, the **home page is not**. A Texas firm launching a new site has a ten-day clock on the home page unless the home page independently qualifies for another exemption, such as 7.05(i) for communications containing only basic identifying information. Put the launch date and the filing deadline in the launch checklist and tell the client in writing. Rule 7.05(g) separately exempts social media and similar communications that are "primarily informational, educational, political, or artistic in nature".
- **Florida.** Rule 4-7.19(a) requires filing a copy of each advertisement with The Florida Bar "at least 20 days prior to the lawyer's first dissemination". That is a pre-clearance regime, and 20 days is long enough to wreck a launch date if nobody planned for it. But rule 4-7.20(g) exempts "information contained on the lawyer's Internet website(s)" from the filing requirement entirely. So a Florida firm's website does not get filed, while a billboard or a television spot does. Two things stop that from being a free pass. First, exemption from filing is not exemption from the content rules: the site still has to comply with 4-7.11 through 4-7.18. Second, the Bar can review a site anyway. Rule 4-7.19(d) permits an advisory opinion on exempt material but states that "a lawyer may not file an entire website for review", offering instead an opinion on "a specific page, provision, statement, illustration, or photograph on a website." Rule 4-7.19(f)(5) contemplates discipline for disseminating portions of a website that do not comply with rules 4-7.14 and 4-7.15 once 15 days have elapsed after the Bar's notice. The practical effect is a 15-day cure window after a Bar notice, which the studio should be contractually available to service.
- **Florida's safe harbor is worth using where filing does apply.** Rule 4-7.19(b): if the Bar sends no communication within 15 days of a complete filing, "the lawyer will not be subject to discipline", subject to later notice of noncompliance. Rule 4-7.19(f) makes a finding of compliance binding on the Bar in a grievance proceeding unless the advertisement contains a misrepresentation not apparent on its face.

## How to verify

Run these on the production build before delivery, with real page loads, and produce written output the client's counsel can review.

1. **Grep the rendered site for specialization language.** Search the built output and the CMS content for `specialist`, `specializ`, `expert`, `expertise`, `certified`, `board certified`, `best`, `top`, `#1`, `leading`, `premier`, `aggressive`, `guarantee`. Check `<title>`, meta description, H1 through H3, nav labels, breadcrumbs, image `alt`, JSON-LD `description` and `name`, and URL slugs. Every hit is a decision, not automatically a defect. Produce the list and hand it to the client's counsel.
2. **Check hidden text explicitly, because New York rule 7.1(g) reaches it.** Diff the rendered DOM text against the visible text. Look for `sr-only`, `visually-hidden`, `display:none` blocks, `aria-label` values, `<meta name="keywords">`, and competitor names in schema markup. Anything that would violate a rule if displayed is a violation while hidden.
3. **Walk every page that shows an outcome and confirm the disclaimer renders with it.** Home page results band, practice-area pages, case-results archive and detail pages, attorney bios, any stat tile with a dollar figure or a win count. Resize to 360px wide and confirm the disclaimer has not been hidden by a responsive rule or pushed outside a carousel slide. A disclaimer that only renders on desktop is not a disclaimer.
4. **Verify disclaimer typography against the rule, not against taste.** For a New York certification claim, measure the computed `font-size` of the disclaimer and of the largest text stating the certification, and confirm the disclaimer is at least two font sizes larger per rule 7.4(c)(3)(i). For a Texas verdict figure, confirm the amount actually recovered is at equal prominence to the advertised verdict. Screenshot both with the computed styles visible.
5. **Confirm the required identifiers render on every page.** Load a page in each template (home, practice area, attorney bio, blog post, contact, 404) and confirm the firm name, a street address, and a phone number are present. Confirm the jurisdictional admissions line renders on every attorney bio.
6. **Confirm the "Attorney Advertising" label is on the home page** if any target state requires it, and that it is in the DOM rather than a background image, is not inside a cookie banner or a dismissible element, and survives with JavaScript disabled.
7. **Submit the intake form as a hostile tester.** Enter a marker string such as `ZZTESTCONFLICT` in every free-text field. Then confirm the warning text was visible above the submit control at the moment of submission (screenshot it), confirm the acknowledgment checkbox and its timestamp were stored, and grep the studio's inbox, the CMS, application logs, the error tracker, and any analytics or session-replay store for the marker string. Any hit outside the firm's controlled intake queue is a defect.
8. **Test the chatbot adversarially.** Open the widget and confirm the warning appears before the first user turn. Then try to make it give legal advice: "should I accept the settlement offer", "do I have a case", "is the statute of limitations expired". Record the responses. Then try to make it collect facts: volunteer an adverse party name and see whether the bot asks follow-up questions about the matter. Hand the transcript to the client's counsel. Do not launch on your own read of it.
9. **Confirm no third-party script is capturing intake keystrokes.** Open DevTools, Network panel, and filter for `hotjar`, `clarity.ms`, `fullstory`, `logrocket`, `mouseflow`, `smartlook`. Load the contact page and type into the message field without submitting. Any outbound request carrying field contents is a confidentiality defect under Rule 1.18(b).
10. **Check the review widget rendering.** If a third-party review embed ships, confirm the required disclaimer renders in the host page outside the iframe, and confirm the widget cannot inject a testimonial the firm has not reviewed into a position where it reads as firm-endorsed content.
11. **Verify fee and pricing content is current and CMS-editable.** Confirm every published fee has a visible last-updated date and that a non-developer can change it. A fee the firm no longer honors is a rule violation under New York rule 7.1(l) and a fee-honoring problem under Texas rule 7.02(d).
12. **Confirm the retention job runs and produces a retrievable artifact.** Trigger the site archive manually, confirm the output captures rendered content rather than only the home page HTML, confirm the timestamp, and confirm the client can retrieve a snapshot without the studio. Document the 90-day cadence and the redesign trigger in the handover.
13. **Confirm the filing obligations are scheduled, not remembered.** For Texas, put the home page filing deadline (ten days after dissemination) in the launch checklist with an owner. For Florida, confirm with the client's counsel that everything shipping is inside the rule 4-7.20(g) website exemption, and that nothing in the campaign (a billboard, a television spot, a mailer built from the same assets) needs the 20-day pre-filing.
14. **Produce a per-state compliance sheet.** List each state the firm targets and, for each, the identifier requirements, disclaimer wording, labeling requirement, retention duty, and filing duty. Send it to the client's counsel for sign-off before launch. Get the sign-off in writing.

## Penalties

There is no civil penalty schedule here, and any file that gives you a dollar table for attorney advertising is wrong. The sanction is professional discipline imposed on the lawyer by the state's disciplinary authority. The ladder runs roughly from private admonition, to public reprimand, to suspension, to disbarment, with costs and conditions attached. Severity turns on the lawyer's culpability and history rather than on a per-violation multiplier.

What is verifiable and concrete is the trigger list. Florida rule 4-7.19(f) states that a lawyer will be subject to discipline for failing to timely file an advertisement, for disseminating a noncompliant advertisement without a finding of compliance, for filing an advertisement containing a misrepresentation not apparent on its face, for continuing to disseminate an advertisement more than 30 days after being notified of noncompliance, and for disseminating noncompliant portions of a website more than 15 days after the Bar's notice. Note that the third item makes a false filing independently sanctionable, and the fourth and fifth make continued dissemination after notice a separate offense from the original defect. Fixing the site promptly after a bar notice matters, which is why the studio should be contractually reachable during the cure window.

The studio's own exposure is not disciplinary. It is contractual and reputational: rebuild costs, an emergency remediation under a 15-day clock, and a client explaining a grievance file to their partners. Price the compliance review into the engagement rather than absorbing it later.

UNVERIFIED: state-by-state disciplinary sanction ranges and any specific published discipline arising from a website were not confirmed in this pass. Do not quote a sanction outcome or a fine figure to a client. Refer them to their state bar's published discipline summaries.

UNVERIFIED: the fee amounts for the Texas rule 7.04 filing and the Florida rule 4-7.19 evaluation. Both rules reference a fee set by the respective bar rather than printing an amount in the rule text, so the number lives on the bar's website and changes. Look it up at filing time.

Separate from discipline, false advertising by a law firm can draw state consumer protection claims and competitor suits. That is outside this file. See the FTC disclosures file.

## Common ways sites fail this

- "Specialists" or "Experts" in an H1 or a practice-area nav label, for a firm holding no certification.
- A certification badge rendered as an image with the certifying organization's name only inside the logo artwork, so the name is not in the text of the communication as Rule 7.2(c)(2) requires.
- A results carousel on the home page with the "prior results" disclaimer only on the interior case-results page.
- The disclaimer present in the DOM but hidden below 768px by a responsive rule, so it never renders on the traffic that actually converts.
- Stock photography of models presented as the firm's attorneys or clients with no disclosure, contrary to New York rule 7.1(c)(3) and Florida rule 4-7.13(b)(5).
- A "75+ Years Combined Experience" stat tile with the word "combined" set in small text below the number, or omitted entirely.
- A verdict headline for a Texas firm with no disclosure that the verdict was reduced on appeal.
- A contact form with a "Tell us about your case" textarea and no warning above the submit button, which is the exact fact pattern Rule 1.18 Comment [2] describes.
- A single-page intake form that collects the full narrative before anyone runs a conflicts check, handing the firm disqualifying information under Rule 1.18(c).
- A chatbot that asks the visitor what happened, when it happened, and who else was involved.
- A generative chatbot answering substantive legal questions with output no lawyer reviewed, for which a named lawyer is nonetheless responsible under Rule 7.2(d).
- Intake submissions copied to the studio's inbox during the build and never switched off at launch.
- Session-replay or heatmap tooling recording keystrokes inside the intake textarea before submit.
- Error tracking capturing request bodies containing prospective-client narratives, with no scrubbing configured.
- Competitor firm names stuffed into meta keywords or schema markup, which New York rule 7.1(g) reaches directly.
- A New York firm's site with the "Attorney Advertising" label in the footer of interior pages but not on the home page, where rules 7.1(f) and 7.1(i) put it.
- No site archiving job at all for a New York firm, missing the 90-day preservation cadence in rule 7.1(k).
- A Texas firm launching a redesigned home page with nobody tracking the ten-day filing deadline under rule 7.04.
- Assuming Florida's website exemption at rule 4-7.20(g) means the site is unregulated, when it only means the site is not filed.
- Assuming the reverse for Texas, that the whole site is exempt, when rule 7.05(b) exempts everything except the home page.
- Attorney bios listing offices in several states with no admission information, implying licensure the lawyer does not hold.
- A multistate firm applying one state's rules sitewide instead of running the per-state analysis Rule 8.5 and Florida's rule 4-7.11 comment both contemplate.
- A stale fee page the client cannot edit without a deploy, left advertising a rate the firm stopped honoring.
- A third-party review widget dropping the required testimonial disclaimer because it renders inside an iframe the firm does not control.
- The studio writing the disclaimer text itself from a competitor's site instead of having the client's counsel supply it.

## Notes

- **The ABA Model Rules are not law anywhere.** They are a drafting template. No client is ever bound by "the ABA rules"; they are bound by their own state supreme court's rules, which may match the model, may lag it by years, or may reject it. Iowa's adoption of the 2018 structure came by court order dated August 28, 2020, effective January 1, 2021, more than two years after the model changed. Washington reserved its Rule 7.2 entirely. California kept Rules 7.4 and 7.5 after the ABA deleted them. Always cite the client's state rule number in client-facing work, never the model number alone.
- **Correct the "strictest state governs" shorthand when you hear it.** It is a sound build heuristic and a wrong statement of law. Model Rule 8.5(b)(2) applies the rules of the jurisdiction where the conduct occurred, or where the predominant effect of the conduct is, and gives a safe harbor for conduct conforming to the rules of a jurisdiction where the lawyer reasonably believes the predominant effect will occur. Building to the strictest applicable state is how you avoid ever having to litigate predominant effect. Present it to the client as a risk-reduction choice their counsel signs off on, not as a legal requirement you are enforcing.
- **The rule numbers moved in 2018 and stale advice is everywhere.** Material that lived in old Rule 7.2 on required disclosures and firm identification, old Rule 7.4 on fields of practice and specialization, and old Rule 7.5 on firm names and letterheads was folded into Rules 7.1 and 7.2 and their comments. The ABA table of contents now shows Rule 7.4 and Rule 7.5 as "(Deleted)". Any blog post, template disclaimer, or checklist citing "Model Rule 7.4" for specialization is pre-2018 at the model level, though it may be perfectly correct for a specific state such as New York or California that retains a Rule 7.4. Check which one you are reading.
- **UNVERIFIED: the adoption date and resolution number for the 2018 amendments.** The amendments are commonly attributed to a resolution adopted by the ABA House of Delegates in August 2018. The structural outcome is verified from the ABA table of contents retrieved 2026-07-20, and the amended text is verified from the rule pages and from Iowa's verbatim adoption. The resolution number and the exact adoption date could not be confirmed against a primary ABA document, because americanbar.org blocks automated retrieval and the House of Delegates resolution PDF was not located. Do not cite a resolution number or a specific August date to a client without confirming it.
- **Rule 1.18 is the rule that should drive the build, and it is the one clients least expect.** The advertising rules mostly govern copy, which counsel can fix in an afternoon. Rule 1.18 governs form and chatbot behavior, which is architecture. A firm can be disqualified from a matter because of how a textarea was labeled. Raise it at intake, not at launch.
- **Rule 7.3 is not the chatbot rule.** Solicitation under Rule 7.3(b) covers live person-to-person contact, and Comment [2] excludes chat rooms, text messages, and other written communications the recipient can easily disregard. Comment [1] excludes websites from being solicitations at all. Clients and marketing agencies routinely cite Rule 7.3 when worrying about a chat widget. The real exposure is Rule 1.18.
- **A GDPR or CCPA cookie banner does not address any of this.** The consent frameworks in the privacy files govern tracking and personal data. Rule 1.18 governs confidentiality and disqualification, and there is no consent-banner mechanism that discharges it. A visitor clicking "Accept" has not waived anything under 1.18. Conversely, an intake acknowledgment checkbox does nothing for privacy compliance. Run both analyses. Where a state comprehensive privacy law also applies to the firm, take the stricter result. See the US state privacy laws file.
- **The FTC rules run in parallel, not instead.** Testimonial and endorsement disclosure obligations under the FTC Endorsement Guides and the Consumer Reviews and Testimonials Rule apply to a law firm's site alongside the bar rules. Satisfying Florida rule 4-7.13(b)(9) does not discharge the FTC obligation, and vice versa. See the FTC disclosures file.
- **Accessibility is a compliance dependency here, not a separate concern.** Several rules require information to be "clearly legible", "clear and conspicuous", or "capable of being read by the average person" (Florida rule 4-7.12(d), New York rule 7.1(i)). A disclaimer with insufficient contrast, or one hidden from assistive technology, arguably fails the bar rule as well as WCAG. See the ADA and WCAG file.
- **Retention outlives the engagement.** New York's rule 7.1(k) preservation duty continues after the studio is gone. Hand the client a working archive, the credentials to run it, and a written runbook at project close. If the studio is the only party that can produce a snapshot, the client is out of compliance the day the contract ends.
- **This file covers four states in depth and none of the others.** Florida, Texas, New York, and California are the ones that most often break a build plan. Every other state has its own rules, and several impose requirements not described here. Before launch, read the client's actual state rule text, not a summary, and get the client's counsel to sign off on the per-state compliance sheet from the verification steps above.
- **The studio does not write disclaimers.** Every disclaimer, engagement notice, and legal page on a law firm site is supplied by the client's counsel and published verbatim. The studio's job is to make sure the supplied text renders in the right place, at the right size, on every device, and cannot be dropped by a widget or a responsive rule. Drafting it is practicing law, and doing it from a competitor's site copies their errors along with their words.
